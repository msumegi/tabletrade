Everyday accounts: chequing and savings

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) is the federal agency that explains banking rights and money tools for consumers. This course points you to those official pages. It does not tell you which bank to choose.

A chequing account is for everyday money tasks. FCAC says people typically use one to deposit pay or other income, pay bills, make debit-card purchases, withdraw cash from an automated teller machine (ATM), and transfer money. Chequing accounts support frequent transactions. They may include a debit card and usually do not earn interest.

A savings account lets you set money aside to use later. FCAC says savings accounts are generally designed for storing money and earning interest rather than for everyday transactions. Frequent use may mean higher transaction fees. Many people use a chequing account for day-to-day spending and a savings account to set money aside.

Interest on a savings account may be compound interest: you earn interest on your deposits, the institution adds that interest to the balance, and then you can earn interest on the new total. How often interest is added, and whether a minimum balance is required, depends on the account agreement. Read that agreement. This course does not calculate a return for you and is not investment advice.

Compare the total cost of an account, not only one fee. Besides a monthly fee, you may pay for extra transactions, some ATM withdrawals, overdraft, or non-sufficient funds (NSF) items. FCAC’s bank-account pages explain those words in more detail.
