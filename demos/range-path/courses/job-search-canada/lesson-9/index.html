Write down the interview while you remember it

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Alis’s page *What to Do After an Interview* (opened 10 November 2026) says that before you forget the details, you should write down what happened. Those notes help you later if you get an offer, and they help you prepare for the next interview if you do not.

Alis suggests noting the names of the interviewer or interviewers; the questions you remember; how you answered; salary figures if they brought them up; and anything else you might need if you are offered the job. Then ask yourself a few review questions: Did you arrive on time and prepared? If it was a virtual interview, did the sound and picture work? Which questions went well? Which would you answer differently next time? Did you connect your skills to what they need? What do you wish you had asked?

Do not take it personally if you do not get the job. Alis says employers often find it hard to choose among several well-qualified people. Someone else may have had more experience, the panel may already have known another candidate, or the fit may simply not have been right. Your notes are for learning, not for blaming yourself.

Keep those notes on your master list, beside the employer’s name and the interview date. If they mentioned a date by which you should hear back, write that date down too, in the same notebook as the job title.

Send a short, specific thank-you

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Alis says that regardless of the outcome it is a good idea to write a brief thank-you for the interviewer’s time and consideration. It can set you apart, remind them of your strongest qualifications, and let you add a fact you missed. Like a resume or cover letter, the note should be tailored: mention something specific from the conversation.

Alis offers a three-part shape:

  • First paragraph: thank them for their time and for telling you about the organization and the position.
  • Middle paragraph: note what you learned, remind them why you are a good fit, and mention any qualification you did not have time to cover. If they raised a concern, you may address it briefly and honestly.
  • Final paragraph: confirm that you are still interested.

You can send the note by email so it arrives quickly. Alis says you may also follow up by regular mail, and that a handwritten note is fine if your writing is easy to read. For most learners in this course, a short email the same day or the next day is enough.

Alis’s interview-preparation page also suggests a thank-you note or email that names two or three reasons you are a good fit. Use reasons you actually discussed or can support — not new inventions.

How you send email, and how you sign out on a shared computer, belongs in *Outlook and Workplace Email Habits*. The words of the thank-you are this lesson’s work.

If you hear nothing — or you hear no

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

If the employer named a date and you have not heard back, alis’s work-search basics page says to note the date and call if nothing arrives by then. If you promised to call on a certain day, put a reminder on your plan and keep the promise.

If there is no offer, alis lists common reasons: someone else had more experience; your answers were not complete; the interviewers wanted to hire someone they already knew; or the fit with the culture was not right. Many interviewers are willing to say why. If you ask for feedback, alis says to make it clear you are not asking them to defend the decision — you want to learn. Suggested questions include how you might improve your resume or interview skills, what you could do better, and whether there might be a future chance. Some people will not answer. Do not argue. Thank them and say goodbye.

Keep looking. Alis says not to assume you have a job until you are told when to start, and to keep following other opportunities even after interviews go well. A thank-you note is not an offer. Update your master list with the status — waiting, no, or (in Lesson 10) an offer to think about — and take care of yourself. Looking for work is tiring. A short walk or a talk with a supportive person is not a waste of the search at all.

Put these after-interview steps in a sensible order.

  1. Write down what happened while you still remember (names, questions, anything they said about pay)
  2. Send a short thank-you that mentions something specific from the conversation
  3. Note the date they said you should hear back, if they named one
  4. Keep looking at other suitable postings until you are told when to start

Try it

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

  1. From a real interview you remember, or from a practice run, write five lines of notes (names, questions, what you would repeat or change).
  2. Draft a three-part thank-you for the Sampletown practice interview, naming two or three honest reasons you fit.
  3. Update your master list with a next step and a date.
  4. Do not send the practice note to a real office.

After the interview — take-home steps

  • Write notes while the conversation is fresh
  • Send a short, specific thank-you (email is fine)
  • Name two or three honest reasons you fit
  • If they gave a date, follow up after that date if you have heard nothing
  • If the answer is no, you may ask polite learning questions — do not argue
  • Keep looking until you have a real start date
