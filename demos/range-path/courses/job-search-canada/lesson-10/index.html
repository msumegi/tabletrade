An offer is more than one pay number

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Alis’s page *Consider These Factors Before Accepting a Job Offer* (opened 10 November 2026) says a job offer is more than a salary figure. It is a commitment that can affect your work, your household, and your well-being. It can be tempting to say yes immediately, especially if you need income soon, but alis asks you to take time to weigh the pros and cons.

Things alis invites you to consider include the base pay; whether there is room for increases; benefits you would actually use (time off, health coverage, a retirement plan, and other items they list); the work itself; how the job fits your longer plan; the people and workplace; hours and on-call expectations; travel time and cost; and whether the work is on-site, remote, or mixed. A “better” job that adds a long drive each way may not be better for your life.

Alis also asks whether the employer has given you the offer in writing. If you ask and they refuse, alis calls that a big red flag. This course will not tell you what to sign. It will tell you to read slowly, write questions down, and ask a person you trust — council staff, Alberta Supports, a settlement worker, or another official helper — before you accept something you do not understand.

Pay notes in a posting or an offer are information. They are not tax advice, payroll advice, or a lesson in *Alberta Employment Standards*. If you need to know about minimum wage, overtime, or vacation pay, use that course or official employment-standards pages, not a guess from this file.

Questions you can write down — and ones this course will not answer

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

You can write down, in your own words: When would I start? What are the hours? Where do I work? What does the pay include? What benefits matter to me? Are there unpaid duties that would add many hours? Is the offer in writing?

Alis says it is natural to feel pushed to accept any offer if you need a change, and asks a useful check: will this job solve the problems that made you start searching? If you are recovering from burnout, flexibility may matter more than a title. If you need a paycheque now, income may come first. Those are personal judgements, not grades in this course.

Alis notes that employers may expect some negotiation and that a first number might not be their last, and that if you are on the fence you should not be afraid to ask for something better. That is general information from their page, not a script. This course will not tell you what dollar amount to request, how to bargain, or whether you should walk away. Ask a person who knows your situation.

Do not use this course for immigration advice about whether you may accept work, or for Employment Insurance advice about leaving a job or starting a new one. Job Bank has separate information for people outside Canada; Service Canada handles SIN and EI questions. If a question is about your legal rights at work, official employment-standards or human-rights pages — or a helper at Alberta Supports (1-877-644-9992, listed on alis on 10 November 2026) — are the right next step.

Accepting, refusing, and finishing this course

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Alis’s work-search basics page says an employer can withdraw an offer, even a written one, until you have accepted it. If you have more than one offer, it also says to accept the job you want, in writing, before you turn the others down. Those are official cautions, not a promise about what every employer will do. If you are juggling dates, ask a helper rather than guessing from a discussion forum.

If you say yes, alis suggests handling a resignation from a current job professionally if that applies to you. If you say no, do it politely and keep your notes — you may meet the same people again.

Then wrap up the search tools you will want next time: keep the master list up to date, keep sample thank-you wording you liked, and keep learning. Alis says work search is a skill you will likely use more than once.

Your capstone for this course is practice: one tailored resume and a short cover email for the fictional Sampletown posting, compared with the model answer. It is not graded. The final exam is a knowledge test. Passing it means you passed that exam (and you should try the capstone). It does not mean a tutor watched you apply for work, and it does not mean you will be hired. When the exam is done, you still have your master list, a sample resume, and official pages you can reopen whenever the next posting appears.

Offers and asking for help — take-home steps

  • An offer is more than one number: hours, travel, benefits, and fit matter too
  • Ask whether the offer is in writing
  • Write questions down; do not rush a yes you do not understand
  • This course does not give legal, immigration, EI, or wage-law advice
  • Ask council staff or Alberta Supports (1-877-644-9992, listed on alis 10 November 2026)
  • Keep looking until you have a start date; a thank-you is not an offer

A tailored resume and cover email (sample only)

Try this before you open the sample answer.

Use the fictional Sampletown Weekend Attendant posting. Write one short resume and one short cover email. This is practice only — not a real application and not a promise of work.

  1. Re-read the sample posting and your Lesson 1 plan (hours, travel, kind of work)
  2. Update the master-skills list if anything is missing
  3. Write a 1- to 2-page resume that uses words from the posting you can honestly support, without tables or columns
  4. Write a short cover email: clear subject, opening, one matching skill with an example, polite close, and a mention of the attached resume
  5. Proofread both (read out loud; watch spell-check misses such as cheque/check)
  6. Save the files with clear names such as YourName_Sampletown_resume and YourName_Sampletown_cover
  7. Do not send them to a real employer or type a SIN anywhere
