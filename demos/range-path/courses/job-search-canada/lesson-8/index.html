An interview is a meeting, not a verdict

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

An interview is a meeting where an employer asks about your skills and you learn about the job. Job Bank’s stand-out page reminds people that being invited is already a step forward: you are part of a smaller group of applicants. This course still cannot promise the job.

Job Bank says to prepare in advance: research the company; think of questions they might ask and prepare answers from your work or education; use the STAR method to structure examples (Situation, Task, Action, Result); practise with friends or family; and on the day, bring copies of your resume and a list of references, and dress in a way that fits the workplace.

Alis treats an interview as a business meeting with two tasks: show that you have the skills to get the job done, and find out whether you are interested in the work. When someone calls to set up a time, you may politely ask whether it is a first screening or a hiring interview, and how many people will be there. Write down names if they offer them.

Alis’s work-search basics page also says to leave questions about pay and benefits until after they offer you the job. That is a timing tip, not a rule of law. If an interviewer raises pay first, you may listen and write the figure down later (Lesson 9). This course will not tell you what number to ask for.

STAR, STARS, and stories you can tell honestly

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Job Bank’s STAR method helps you turn a memory into a clear example: what was going on (Situation), what you needed to do (Task), what you did (Action), and what happened (Result). Alis uses a similar method called STARS, which adds Skills — name the skill the story shows.

Pick examples that match the posting. For Sampletown, a useful story might be greeting a long line at a volunteer skate sale (situation), keeping the line moving without rushing people (task), calling the next person and answering where to try on skates (action), and finishing the morning without a backup at the door (result). The skill is clear, calm communication.

Write two stories on the interview practice sheet before you try them out loud. Alis says to practise by saying answers out loud, and to try a practice interview with a friend while you notice whether you sound rushed or unclear. You do not need a perfect performance. You need examples you can remember when you are nervous.

Be ready to explain a gap or a weakness without inventing a history. Alis says to be prepared to explain perceived weaknesses and the steps you have taken to address them. Honest and short is better than a long excuse.

Interview practice sheet (sample) Sample file. Not private data.

Alis groups interview habits in four steps. Put them in order.

  1. Prepare (research, review your resume, plan examples and questions)
  2. Practise (say answers out loud; try a practice interview)
  3. Present yourself well (be neat, on time, and ready to listen)
  4. Participate (listen, answer thoughtfully, and follow up later)

The day of the interview, and your questions

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Alis’s four habits continue on the day: present yourself well — be neat, on time, and dressed in a way that fits the workplace; turn off your cellphone; greet people politely. Alis also mentions avoiding scents and waiting to sit until you are offered a chair. Participate by listening closely, answering thoughtfully, and taking brief notes if that helps you. It is all right to steer the conversation gently toward a skill the job needs.

Prepare a short list of questions for them about the work itself — for example, what a typical Saturday looks like, or how new attendants learn the closing checklist. Alis says always to word questions politely. Alis’s *Work Search Basics* page also says to leave questions about pay and benefits until after they offer you the job.

Alis also says you should understand your rights about what kinds of questions are appropriate, and the work-search basics page notes that some personal questions are not allowed. This course is not legal advice and will not give you a full list of prohibited questions. If a question worries you, you may say you would like to think about it, and later ask council staff or read official human-rights information. Do not use this course as a courtroom script.

For a phone or online interview, alis says to pick a time and place where you can speak comfortably, and to have your resume, a pen, paper, and your questions nearby. Practise with the sheet rather than memorizing a speech word for word.

After the interview comes Lesson 9. Do not assume you have the job until an employer tells you when to start (alis). Keep looking at other suitable postings.

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

  1. Fill two STAR or STARS examples on the interview practice sheet, using the Sampletown posting.
  2. Write two polite questions about the work (not about pay).
  3. Say one example out loud, or practise with a classmate if you have one.
  4. Pack, on paper, what you would bring: resume copies and a reference list you have permission to use.

Interview preparation — take-home steps

  • Research the organization and reread your resume
  • Plan STAR (and STARS) examples that match the posting
  • Practise out loud; bring resume copies and a permitted reference list
  • Be neat, on time, and ready to listen; turn off your phone
  • Prepare polite questions about the work; alis suggests leaving pay until there is an offer
  • This course is not legal advice about interview questions
  • Do not assume you have the job until you are told when to start
