Start a new message

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Microsoft’s basic Outlook page (opened 10 November 2026) says you create a new email from any mail folder, such as your Inbox, by choosing New Email. The keyboard shortcut Microsoft lists is Ctrl+Shift+M. When you are done composing, you choose Send.

Open Mail first so you can see the Inbox. Then choose New Email. A blank message window opens. It has a place for recipients, a subject line, and a large area for the words you want to send. Screens differ in classic Outlook, new Outlook, the web, and mobile apps, but those three parts are the ones you need today: who the message is for, what it is about, and what you want to say.

In the To box, type the address of the person who must read and act on the message. For practice, use the sample names in Outlook-Workplace-Sample-Messages-Sample-Only.txt, such as Jordan Lee at the Sampletown address we wrote. Do not send practice messages to a real workplace inbox unless your tutor says the practice account is set up for that.

Leave Cc and Bcc for Lesson 3. Today you are sending a simple note to one person. If you click New Email by mistake, close the window and choose No if Outlook asks whether to save a draft — or save the draft if you want to come back. Closing without Send does not deliver the message.

Use a tutor-approved practice account. Never type a real workplace password into this course. The practice screens are drawings labelled “Practice screen — not the real Outlook.” They will ask you to tap New Email and Send, not to hand over a password.

If New Email does not appear where you expect, look at the top of the Mail view. Microsoft says you start from any mail folder, so make sure you are in Mail and not in Calendar. Lesson 1 showed that switching views changes what you see; it does not create a new message for you.

Write a subject and a short body

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Microsoft’s page on writing great email (opened 10 November 2026) says the key is to empathize with your recipients. Start with the subject. Make it descriptive and action-oriented so the other person can tell what the message is about before they open it. Microsoft’s example names a group and an action, such as asking managers to send dates. Useful prefixes include FYI: when the note is only for information and Action Required when the person must do something.

A subject such as “Question” or “Hi” does not help. “Saturday hall setup — can you confirm by Thursday?” tells Jordan what the note is about and what you need. If you are answering someone later in a long thread and the topic has changed, Microsoft says to change the subject. You will use Reply in Lesson 3; the same subject rule still applies.

Keep the body short and to the point. Organize the content from most important to least. Put questions or action items on separate lines so they stand out. Microsoft also suggests considering bold for important information, and putting people who must respond on the To line — which is where your one recipient belongs today.

Write the way you would speak politely at a front desk. Use Canadian spelling (*centre*, *colour*, *practise* as a verb). You do not need fancy stationery, unusual fonts, or a long story about your morning. If you cannot give a full answer yet, Microsoft says to let the sender know you are looking into the issue and will respond by a certain time. That habit also works when you are the one writing first: if you still need a fact, say so and name when you will follow up.

Read the message before you send it. Microsoft lists that as the first “do.” A thirty-second read catches a missing date, a wrong name, or a sentence that sounds sharper than you meant.

Send the message, then check that it went

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

When the To line, the subject, and the body look right, choose Send. Microsoft’s basic steps end there: compose, then Send. You do not need extra buttons for a simple practice note.

After you send, look for the message in Sent Items if your screen shows that folder. Microsoft’s Bcc page (opened 10 November 2026) notes that sent messages are stored by default in the Sent Items folder in classic Outlook for Windows. If you do not see Sent Items, ask a tutor — folder names can differ slightly, and you should not invent a second send “just in case.” Sending twice can confuse the other person.

If you realize you forgot a sentence, you can send a short follow-up. Microsoft warns against expecting a quick response to a long message and against sending a follow-up less than a day after the first note. For a missing time or a missing attachment (Lesson 4), a brief second message is kinder than silence. For “did you see my email?” sent ten minutes later, wait or use a phone call if you need an immediate answer. Microsoft says that if you want an immediate response, email is not the best tool — phone or an instant message can be clearer.

Do not mark every new message as High Importance. Microsoft says to use that marking sparingly, because people learn to ignore it. A clear subject does more work than a red exclamation mark.

Save a draft if you are not ready to send. Coming back to a draft is safer than sending an unfinished note, especially if you feel rushed. Lesson 4 will add tone and attachments. Today, a clear To line, a clear subject, a short body, and Send are enough.

Worked example 1. Confirming Saturday setup

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Worked example 1. Confirming Saturday setup

Problem

Alex needs to tell Jordan that Saturday from 9:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. still works.

Steps

  1. Alex opens Mail, chooses New Email, and puts Jordan Lee on the To line.
  2. The subject is “Saturday hall setup — I can help from 9:00 to 10:00.”
  3. The body says “I can help set up tables this Saturday from 9:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m.” and “Please let me know if the plan changes.”

Result

Alex reads the note once, chooses Send, and later checks Sent Items instead of sending the same note again.

Worked example 2. Fixing a weak subject

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Worked example 2. Fixing a weak subject

Problem

Priya starts a message with the subject “Question” and a long paragraph about chairs, tables, and a heater.

Steps

  1. Before sending, Priya rereads Microsoft’s subject advice and changes the subject to “Action Required: extra chairs for Saturday’s market.” Priya moves the chair question to its own line and saves the heater note for a separate message, because it is a different topic.

Result

The new version is easier for Jordan to scan.

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

  1. Download the sample-messages file if you have not already.

Discussion (optional)

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

If you are in a study group: rewrite one weak subject (“Hi,” “Question,” or “Update”) into a clearer line. Compare your versions.

Lesson 2 — Write and send

  • From the Inbox (or another mail folder), choose New Email. Shortcut: Ctrl+Shift+M.
  • Put the person who must act on the To line.
  • Write a descriptive, action-oriented subject (FYI: or Action Required can help).
  • Keep the body short. Most important point first. Questions on their own lines.
  • Read the message, then choose Send.
  • Check Sent Items rather than sending the same note twice.
  • Use a practice account. Never type a real workplace password into this course.
