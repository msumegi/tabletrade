Rows, columns, and the cell where they meet

Statistics Canada says a data table is a structured arrangement of data in rows and columns. It is used to display a large amount of numerical information in an organized way.

Think of a simple list of community suppers:

  • A column runs up and down. Each column has a header that names one kind of information, such as “Date,” “Meal,” or “Tickets sold.”
  • A row runs left to right. Each row is usually one item or one group, such as one supper or one age group.
  • A cell is the box where a row and a column meet. The number or words in that box belong to that row and that heading.

Statistics Canada’s steps for reading a table start with the title. The title should tell you what the table is about, who or where it covers, and often the time period. Next, identify the column headers. Then check the source and any notes. Then identify the row stubs — the labels down the side that name each row.

To find one fact, locate the row you care about and the column you care about, and read the cell where they intersect. In Statistics Canada’s example, the prevalence of disability for people aged 45 to 64 is found where that age-group row meets the “Prevalence of disability” column.

Tables can also help you compare numbers and notice patterns. They are a foundation for charts and other pictures of data. You can keep a table in any tool — or even on paper. The idea of rows, columns, and cells does not belong to one brand of software.
