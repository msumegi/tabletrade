Lesson 5 of 5

Put the pieces together

You now have a simple way to approach any list, table, or chart, in any program or on paper.

  1. Read the title. What is this about, and for what time and place?
  2. Read the headings (or the labels on a chart). What kind of information is in each column or on each axis?
  3. Check the units and whether a number is a total or an average.
  4. Check the source and any notes or quality marks. Statistics Canada, for example, uses letters such as E to mean an estimate should be interpreted with caution, and F to mean an estimate is too unreliable to publish. Other tables may use different notes.
  5. Find the cell — or the bar, line, or slice — that answers your question.
  6. Look for a pattern, but do not invent a story the numbers do not support.

Skills for Success numeracy includes identifying the question you need to answer and checking whether a result makes sense. If a total looks far too big or too small, look again before you share it.

When you keep your own list, use the same habits: a clear title, consistent headings, noted units and dates, and a source. Save the file with a name that will still make sense next month.

This is not a data-analyst course, not a statistics credit, and not a repeat of Excel basics or Excel next steps. Those Excel courses teach cells, tables, sort and filter, and creating charts in that program. Use them if you want the software steps. Use this course when you need to understand the data itself.
