Keep a clean, readable list

A list is easier to trust when it is tidy. Statistics Canada’s table-reading steps are also a good checklist when you make a list.

Give the list a title that matches what is inside it. Note the time period — a week, a month, or a year — so nobody mixes old numbers with new ones. Use clear column headings. Put the same kind of information under each heading. If one column is “Tickets sold,” do not put a person’s phone number in that column.

Watch the units. Statistics Canada reminds readers to check whether numbers are in ones, thousands, or millions, and whether a value is a total or an average. If your list is about dollars, say so. If it is about people, say so. Write totals on a separate row so they are not confused with a single item.

Name your source, or note that you collected the numbers yourself. Statistics Canada says you should not trust a table that does not clearly show where the data came from. Checking the reliability of a source is part of reading data well.

The official data journey includes a step to explore, clean, describe, and understand the data before you analyze it. Cleaning, in plain language, means fixing obvious problems: a missing heading, a date written two different ways, or a blank that should have been a zero or a note.

Skills for Success Digital includes storing and organizing digital information in a logical way with files, folders, and labels. Give your list a clear file name and keep it where you and your group can find it.

You do not need Excel’s sort or filter buttons for this lesson. A clean list on paper or in any app is enough.
