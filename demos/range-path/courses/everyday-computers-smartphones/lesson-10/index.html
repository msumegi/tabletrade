Start with the simple checks

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

When a device “does not work,” the cause is often something small. This course is not a tech-support contract and it will not walk you through a force-restart on a shared phone — those steps differ by model, and guessing at buttons can make things worse. Try this calm list first.

  1. Power. Is a charger or power cable connected? A dark screen may mean an empty battery, not a broken computer. If nothing happens after you connect power and press the power button once, stop and ask for help.
  2. Wake and lock. Press the power button or a key to wake the screen. If you see a lock screen, sign in the way you already do (Lesson 2). On Windows, Windows key + L locks the computer without closing your work; signing in again should bring that work back.
  3. Volume and sound. If a video or call seems silent, look for a volume key or a volume control in Settings rather than tapping at random.
  4. Internet. If a page will not load, check whether Wi-Fi is on and whether you joined the network you meant (Lesson 5). Get Cyber Safe says you can turn Wi-Fi off when you do not need it — so it may be off on purpose.
  5. Settings and search. Open Settings (Lesson 1 and 2) and search for the word you need, such as volume or Wi-Fi, instead of browsing every menu.

If the screen is frozen, do not keep pressing keys harder. On Windows, Microsoft lists Ctrl + Alt + Delete as a way to open a security screen where you can lock, switch user, sign out, or open other tools. Ask a tutor before you sign out of a shared machine, because unsaved work can be lost. On a phone, ask staff rather than holding unfamiliar button combinations.

Who to ask, and what to do if a device is missing

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

You are not expected to fix everything yourself. Good people to ask:

  • A tutor or library staff member, for a machine in that building.
  • A family member you already trust, for a home device — without handing them every password (Lesson 9: never share your password).
  • Your phone or internet service provider, for a lost phone or a network that will not come back after the simple checks.
  • Local police, if a device is stolen.

Get Cyber Safe says you should never leave a phone in a vehicle or unattended in public, and you should keep a record of the make and model. If the device goes missing, their steps are:

  1. Contact your service provider. They can help deactivate the device and restore it to factory settings, and they can place the phone on a national blacklist so it will not work on any Canadian wireless network.
  2. Report it to local law enforcement. If you can find the device’s coordinates, give that information to the authorities. Never attempt to recover stolen property by yourself.

Those steps are safety information, not legal advice. This course cannot blacklist a phone for you and cannot contact police for you.

Be careful about “help” that appears on the screen without you asking: a pop-up that names a phone number, a person in a café who offers to “fix it if you unlock it,” or a message that asks for remote access. Use the people and numbers you already know, or that staff give you.

Put these calm checks in a sensible order when a device will not do what you expect.

  1. Ask a tutor, staff member, or your provider if it still does not work
  2. Check the charger or power cable, then try to wake the screen
  3. Check Settings or Wi-Fi if you need the internet
  4. Unlock the screen the way you already do, if a lock appears

Put the everyday skills on one list

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

You now have a short set of habits that work on a phone and on a computer:

  • Name the device and find Settings (Lesson 1).
  • Power on, lock the screen, and use auto-lock (Lesson 2).
  • Tap, scroll, go Home or Back, and use a few Windows shortcuts (Lesson 3).
  • Find a file in File Explorer or the Files app, and save with a clear name (Lesson 4).
  • Join a known Wi-Fi network and treat public Wi-Fi with care (Lesson 5).
  • Use official app stores and say yes to updates (Lesson 6).
  • Search, then check the address and the security status (Lesson 7).
  • Send a plain practice message, and skip surprise links or attachments (Lesson 8).
  • Use a strong, unique passphrase and never share it (Lesson 9).

The capstone below asks you to try five of those skills on a real device, with staff nearby for Wi-Fi. You do not have to be fast. You do not have to own a new phone. A library or council computer still counts. If a brand’s labels differ from a practice screen, ask — screens differ, and this course said that from the first page.

When you finish the capstone, take the final exam. It is multiple choice, it is the only graded piece, and the pass mark is 70 percent. You have two attempts. The certificate is a sample of completion; it is not college credit and not a tech-support licence.

Worked example 1. A laptop that will not “turn on”

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Worked example 1. A laptop that will not “turn on”

Problem

Avery’s library laptop looks dead.

Steps

  1. Avery checks that the power cable is seated at both ends and waits a moment.
  2. The lock screen appears.
  3. Avery signs in and finds the practice file from Lesson 4.

Result

No extra buttons were held down, and no pop-up “support” number was called.

Worked example 2. A phone left on a bus

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Worked example 2. A phone left on a bus

Problem

Avery realises a phone may have been left on a bus.

Steps

  1. Following Get Cyber Safe, Avery contacts the mobile provider to ask about deactivating the device and does not go looking for a stranger who might have it.
  2. Avery also plans to tell local police.

Result

Avery does not text the password to a number that later messages “we found your phone.”

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Run the simple-check list on the device in front of you (without changing other people’s settings):

  1. Confirm power.
  2. Wake and lock the screen once.
  3. Find Settings.
  4. If something still fails, ask a tutor rather than guessing.

When something goes wrong — take-home steps

  • Check power and the charger first
  • Wake or lock the screen the way you already do (Windows key + L locks a PC)
  • Check volume, then Wi-Fi and Settings
  • Ask a tutor or staff before you hold unfamiliar buttons
  • Lost phone: contact your provider and police; do not recover stolen property yourself
  • Do not call phone numbers that appear in surprise pop-ups
  • This course is not a repair service

Everyday-skills checklist (capstone)

Try this before you open the sample answer.

Try these five skills on a real device you are allowed to use. A library or council computer counts. Ask staff to stand nearby before you join Wi-Fi. Do not type a real password, PIN, or Wi-Fi key into this course — only into the real device, if you need to.

  1. Lock the screen (phone power button / auto-lock, or Windows key + L), then sign in again
  2. Find a file you saved, or save a short practice note with a clear name and find it again
  3. Connect to a known Wi-Fi network with staff nearby (skip this step if staff ask you not to change the network)
  4. Open a browser and look at the address bar and security status on a page a tutor agrees to
  5. Send a short practice message to a tutor or practice address, then sign out if you are on a shared machine
