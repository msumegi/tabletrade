Texts, chat apps, and email

About 3 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

People send short notes in more than one way.

A text (SMS) uses a phone number and the Messages app that came with many phones. A chat or messaging app (the name differs by brand) sends notes over the internet once you have an account. Email uses an email address and a mail app or a website such as Gmail. Skills for Success names email as one of the everyday digital tools people use to communicate. This lesson stays with those everyday moves. It does not replace *Email & Online Safety Basics in Canada*, and it does not teach Outlook — that course covers workplace inbox habits.

Each channel has a place. A text is handy for “I am at the desk.” Email is better when you want a subject line, a longer note, or a record you can search later. Get Cyber Safe says not to use text messages to send personal or sensitive information, and to avoid sending sensitive information by SMS or email when you are dealing with unexpected messages. If a note contains a health detail, a password, or banking information, stop and ask whether you should use a more private method — or wait and speak to the person.

On a shared or library computer, open only a mail account staff say you may use (often a practice account). Sign out when you finish if they ask you to. Do not open someone else’s inbox to “have a look.”

Write a short message you mean to send

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

The labels differ by app, but the idea is the same: say who the note is for, what it is about, write the words, then send.

Gmail’s official write-and-send help (opened 10 November 2026) describes one common computer path: go to Gmail, click Compose at the top left, add recipients in the To field, add a subject, write your message, and click Send at the bottom. Drafts are saved automatically in a label named Drafts. You can also add Cc or Bcc recipients if you need copies. Other mail apps use different button names, but a tutor can point to the same four parts: who, subject, message, send.

Keep a first practice note short and plain. For this course, write to a tutor or to a practice address staff give you — not to a real employer, bank, or government office. Say who you are, why you are writing, and what you would like the person to know. You do not need fancy formatting. You do not need to attach a file today.

If you attach something later, remember Get Cyber Safe’s warning about surprise files (Chapter 3). On the sending side, attach only a file you created and that the other person expects. Double-check the To field before you tap Send so the note does not go to the wrong person. If your app offers Undo send for a few seconds, that can rescue a slip — Gmail describes an Undo option after sending — but do not count on it.

Think before you tap a link or an attachment

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Get Cyber Safe explains that phishing can arrive as email, as a phone call, or as smishing (a text). The best everyday defence they list is to stay alert: never respond to unexpected messages from unknown numbers, avoid sending sensitive information by SMS or email, and think twice before clicking links or opening attachments.

Their “think before you text” list is specific:

  • Do not use texts to send personal or sensitive information.
  • Never open attachments from unknown or untrusted sources.
  • Never click the links or call the phone numbers in suspicious messages — even if they say they are from your bank or mobile provider.

Malware can also reach a phone through internet downloads, text attachments, or Bluetooth transfers, Get Cyber Safe notes. That is one more reason to skip a surprise file and to turn Bluetooth off when you are not using it (their phones-and-tablets page asks you to do that so other people cannot try to pair with your device).

If a message looks urgent (“your account will close today”), that urgency is a reason to slow down, not to hurry. Use the habit from Lesson 7: open the organisation from its real home page or a saved bookmark if you still need to check something. The longer safety course has more examples of scams. This lesson only asks you to treat unexpected links, attachments, and phone numbers as things you can ignore until you have asked a person you trust.

Worked example 1. A practice note to a tutor

Problem

Riley opens a practice Gmail account on a library computer, clicks Compose, types the tutor’s practice address in To, writes the subject “Practice note from Riley,” and writes two sentences: who Riley is and that the practice message from Lesson 8 is done.

Steps

  1. Riley checks the To field, clicks Send, and signs out.

Result

No password, address, or health detail is in the note.

Worked example 2. A text that asks you to call a “bank” number

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

Worked example 2. A text that asks you to call a “bank” number

Problem

Riley gets a text that says a bank account is locked and lists a phone number to call at once.

Steps

  1. The number is not one Riley saved.
  2. Following Get Cyber Safe, Riley does not call the number in the text and does not tap the link.

Result

If Riley still wants to check the account, Riley uses a phone number or website already known to be the bank’s, or asks a tutor how to find the official contact later.

Try it

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

  1. Open the mail app or website staff say you may use (a practice account if they offer one).
  2. Start a new message (Compose in Gmail on a computer).
  3. Address it to a tutor or practice address — not to a real workplace or bank.
  4. Add a short subject and two plain sentences.
  5. Check the To field, send, and sign out if you are on a shared machine.
  6. Do not open unexpected attachments and do not send private details.

Optional discussion (council study group)

About 2 minutes to read, plus a short practice.

When is a short text enough, and when is an email with a subject line easier to find later?

Everyday messages — take-home steps

  • Text, chat, and email are different channels; email has a subject you can search later
  • Gmail on a computer: Compose, To, subject, message, Send
  • Practise with a tutor or practice address only
  • Do not send sensitive information by SMS (Get Cyber Safe)
  • Do not open attachments from unknown sources
  • Do not tap links or call numbers in suspicious messages
  • Sign out of mail on a shared computer if staff ask you to
