Lesson 3 of 5

Qualified interpreters are not “a friend who knows some signs”

A professional interpreter’s job is to relay communication between people who do not share a language. Finishing this course does not give you that skill.

CAD-ASC’s interpreting page says qualified professional interpreting helps non-Deaf people communicate with Deaf people. It supports using qualified professional interpreters who belong to the interpreter associations named on that page. It does not consider family, friends, acquaintances, social workers, teachers, educational assistants, ministers, or others to be qualified professional interpreters.

CAD-ASC also says interpreters (except Deaf interpreters and Deaf intervenors) are not experts or consultants on Deaf issues. They facilitate communication. Expertise on Deaf issues and Deaf culture comes from Deaf people themselves.

If your workplace, clinic, or council meeting needs signed-language access, arrange a qualified interpreter. Do not ask a co-worker’s child, a neighbour, or “someone who took a short course” to interpret an important conversation. That can leave out information and put private details in the wrong hands.

Skills for Success collaboration includes valuing diversity and working respectfully with people who have different abilities and ways of communicating. Government of Canada accessibility guidance also says organizations should consider offering materials in ASL, LSQ, or Indigenous sign languages, and should plan time for interpreter requests.

If you are speaking with a Deaf person through an interpreter, talk with the person, not about them as if they were not there. Wait for the interpreter to finish. This course still does not make you that interpreter.
